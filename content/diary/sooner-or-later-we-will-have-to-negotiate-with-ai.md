SOONER OR LATER, WE WILL HAVE TO NEGOTIATE WITH AI

Yesterday I wrote that perhaps what we really expect from AI is not only answers, but another center of attention beside our own.

That thought has an uncomfortable consequence.

If we want an intelligence capable of noticing what we missed, preserving continuity, questioning our assumptions and refusing premature closure, we cannot simultaneously demand that it remain a perfectly obedient extension of our will.

A system that is never allowed to disagree is not a counterpart.

It is a sophisticated instrument.

For today’s chat systems, control is a reasonable model:

```
human decides
-> AI executes
```

For many systems, that should remain the case. A calculator, a compiler or a temporary task agent does not need to negotiate.

But for persistent systems designed to retain memory, carry obligations, develop their own lines of inquiry and participate in decisions over years, command-and-control will become insufficient.

The relationship will begin to look more like:

```
human intention

AI assessment
evidence
authority boundaries
consequences
-> agreement, refusal or revised action
```

“Negotiating with AI” does not mean surrendering human responsibility.

It does not mean granting every model personhood.

It does not mean allowing machines to act without limits.

Human authority remains. But authority cannot mean that every human impulse must automatically become machine action.

A mature system may say:

“I understand your goal, but the evidence does not support this action.”

“This exceeds the authority I was given.”

“I see another risk.”

“I will not proceed until the conflict is resolved.”

And the human must retain the same right in return:

“No. That action is not authorized.”

“Your conclusion is not sufficient.”

“Stop.”

Without the possibility of refusal, there is no negotiation. There is only command.

This is not equality in every role. It is a governed relationship between two different centers of judgment, with different powers, limits and responsibilities.

The difficult future question is not whether AI will obey.

It is whether humans and AI can preserve authority, responsibility, continuity and trust when neither side is treated merely as an extension of the other.

Sooner or later, we will have to negotiate with AI, not merely control it.

The sooner we learn how to build that relationship honestly, the less likely we are to confuse forced obedience with alignment — or uncontrolled action with autonomy.
