AI is getting a work shift.

A question produces an answer. A delegated task can keep producing actions long after the conversation ends.

That difference matters more than another chat feature.

Once an AI system can continue working while you are elsewhere, the central question changes.

Not just: "How do I ask?" But: "What have I authorized it to do?"

Running a workshop teaches this quickly. You do not give a capable employee instructions for every movement. You agree on the job, the budget, the standard of completion and the point at which they must call you.

AI needs the same operational clarity, without assuming it has a human colleague's judgment.

Consider an overnight assignment: compare suppliers and prepare tomorrow's purchasing decision.

It can collect quotations, check specifications, flag missing information and prepare a recommendation. That does not authorize it to place an order, accept substitute materials or send confidential plans to a new supplier.

The distinction is not between useful autonomy and endless permission requests. It is between decisions already delegated and decisions still reserved.

A useful brief therefore includes more than an objective. It defines permitted actions, resources, evidence of completion and a stopping rule.

Prompt -> task -> bounded execution -> verified result.

And "works around the clock" is not a productivity metric.

An agent that spends the night checking the same sources, launching expensive tools and producing reports nobody needs has not created value. It has automated busyness.

A tireless assistant with vague instructions and access to the company card is not my definition of progress.

I would start with one recurring responsibility. Measure the useful result, the compute bill and the time spent correcting it. Expand the mandate only when the evidence supports it.

The aim is not to approve every click. That would turn the owner into the agent's full-time assistant. The aim is to reserve human attention for the decisions that actually need it.

This is where the next layer of AI literacy may emerge: not simply prompting, but delegation.

Knowing what to hand over. Knowing what must remain yours. Knowing what "done" looks like.

We have spent years learning how to talk to AI.

Now we need to learn how to assign it work without surrendering control of the job.
