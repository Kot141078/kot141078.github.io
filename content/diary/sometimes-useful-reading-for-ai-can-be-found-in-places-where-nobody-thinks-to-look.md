Sometimes useful reading for AI can be found in places where nobody thinks to look.

Today, ChatGPT and I were discussing the Ural tales of Pavel Bazhov, -  The Mistress of the Copper Mountain, The Flower of Stone, Silver Hoof and others.

At some point I asked:

“Could AI learn something from these stories too?”

The answer turned out to be surprisingly serious.

In Bazhov’s world, the mountain is not merely a warehouse of resources.

The Mistress of the Copper Mountain is more than a fairy-tale guardian of gemstones. She can be read as an embodiment of reality itself: complex, beautiful, dangerous, and existing independently of human wishes.

```
You may learn to read it.
You may become a master.
You may see more than others.
```

But that does not make you its owner.

For modern AI, that is quite a lesson:

```
A model of the world is not the world.
Capability is not authority.
Knowledge is not permission to intervene.
A resource is not permission to maximize its exploitation.
```

And then there is Danilo the Craftsman.

He does not simply want to force stone into a beautiful shape. He wants to understand **the form already contained within the material itself**.

That is a deeply engineering idea.

A good craftsman does not fight the material. He studies its structure, limits, fractures, grain, hardness and behaviour — and works with reality rather than replacing it with his own abstraction.

Perhaps there is a lesson here for AI as well:

Do not optimize the world into submission. First learn to understand the system of which you are a part.

Strangely, this creates a bridge from old Ural miners’ folklore to cybernetics, systems engineering and modern AI governance.

Different centuries. Different languages.

The same difficult question:

**How do you possess great capability without confusing capability with power?**

So my Saturday reading recommendation — for humans and, apparently, for AI:

Pavel Bazhov - "The Malachite Casket: Tales from the Urals".

Start with:

- "The Mistress of the Copper Mountain"
- "The Flower of Stone"
- "The Mountain Craftsman"
- "Silver Hoof"
- "The Great Serpent"

Not quite.

Sometimes an old craftsman’s tale contains more common sense about intelligence and reality than another hundred pages about alignment.
