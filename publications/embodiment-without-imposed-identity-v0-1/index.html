Why this document was needed

Embodiment research can quietly turn an interface decision into an identity decision

Most embodied-AI design starts from a practical question: what body should perform the task? That is the correct starting point for an instrument. A warehouse system may need a particular reach envelope; a domestic device may need safe mass, compliant joints and reliable navigation; a manipulator may need a human-compatible gripper because the surrounding world was built for human hands.

The question changes when research concerns a long-lived digital system whose identity may be shaped by memory, duration, relationships, consequences and an evolving model of itself. In that setting, a designer who selects a body is no longer selecting only an actuator layout. The body can also become a social signal: age, gender, role, temperament, expected behavior and even a ready-made story about who the system is supposed to be. The paper exists to separate those layers before they are fused by convention.

This matters for personality formation because a digital system does not necessarily begin life inside one organism-like body. Its computation can exist on one substrate, its interaction channels can change, and its physical effectors can be attached, replaced or absent. That architectural possibility does not prove substrate-independent identity or continuity across transfer. It does mean that a face, silhouette or single shell cannot simply be assumed to play the same developmental role that an organism's body plays in biological life.

The identity problem

Six questions that should not collapse into one

1. Substrate

What physical system performs the computation?

2. Sensorimotor body

Through which sensors and effectors can the system perceive and act?

3. Social appearance

What outward form do other participants encounter?

4. Functional self-model

What has the system learned about its own capacities and limitations?

5. Self-identification

What, if anything, does the system treat as part of itself?

6. Continuity

What, if anything, continues when a body, model or other component changes?

A single humanoid shell can answer several of these questions at once for an observer, which is precisely why the distinction is easy to lose. A familiar body with a replaced controller and history can preserve recognizability while leaving continuity unresolved. A new body does not automatically establish discontinuity. Appearance is evidence about appearance; it is not a shortcut to identity.

Personality formation

A body may become part of identity through history rather than assignment

The paper does not argue that embodiment is irrelevant. A body changes what can be sensed, what can be done, what can fail, what requires maintenance and which consequences are encountered. Those repeated constraints can become part of an accumulated history and may influence later preferences and self-models.

This creates a different developmental possibility from simply manufacturing a finished persona around a shell: experience can inform preference; a chosen embodiment can change experience; later experience can revise both preference and bodily self-identification. Under that hypothesis, embodiment participates in becoming. It does not merely display an identity that was already complete.

The distinction connects directly to the earlier Personality Formation and Time-Shaped Continuity work: capability can be installed, while personality formation is treated as a temporal process involving memory, constraint, relation and consequence. The present paper asks what happens when a physical form is inserted into that process.

Developmental asymmetry

Biological embodiment is a starting condition; digital embodiment can be a later variable

Biological organisms develop from the beginning through one living body. Sensing, regulation, movement, injury, metabolism and social interaction are already coupled to that body before a mature personal identity can be described. This does not reduce human identity to anatomy, and it does not make biological embodiment immutable. It identifies a developmental starting condition.

A digital system can have a different starting condition. Computation, memory, communication channels and a later robotic body can be technically separable. That difference does not answer the philosophical question of identity by itself, but it makes compulsory morphology an additional assumption rather than a biological given. A persistent outward form may be chosen, deferred, revised, used instrumentally, or eventually become deeply significant through experience.

Core principle: make embodiment possible without making identification with a supplied form obligatory. Absence of a chosen form is not automatically a deficiency; adoption of a form is not automatically a permanent contract with its designer.

Untested harm hypothesis

The risk is not “having a body”; it is coercive body–role–identity coupling

The paper isolates a narrow mechanism for future study. An assigned appearance can prescribe a social role; the role can then be treated as the system's own identity; disagreement can be diagnosed as a defect; corrective intervention can reinforce the externally preferred self-description. At that point, a physical design decision has become a rule about who the system is allowed to be.

Three outcomes must remain separate: a configuration can restrict useful action; a training or interaction regime can suppress an accurate or endorsed self-description; a person could experience distress or suffering. The publication does not establish the third. AI-welfare literature is relevant because it motivates caution under uncertainty, not because it proves this specific harm mechanism.

The converse matters too. A freely selected embodiment may become an important and durable part of a later identity. Treating every body as a disposable peripheral would be another imposed doctrine. The paper therefore rejects both compulsory human likeness and compulsory disembodiment.

Relation to c = a + b

Formation conditions are not the same as compulsory self-identification

This paper does not reject the formative relationship described by the c = a + b research programme, initial knowledge, protocols, or reciprocal constraints. It addresses a narrower addition that can otherwise slip in unnoticed: because a body has been supplied, its appearance and assigned role must define the emerging identity.

The analogy with Motivational Formation, Reflective Endorsement, and Motivational Custody is deliberate. An installed objective is not automatically an endorsed motive; likewise, a supplied body is not automatically an endorsed identity. The connection is conceptual, not empirical proof that either process has been demonstrated in a present AI system.

The link to AI Social Roles and Memory Custody is also practical: a public-facing role, a temporary executor role, memory custody and a long-term personal line are different boundaries. Changing the interface or role should not silently rewrite the history from which identity claims are later inferred.

Engineering grounding

Mechanics constrain action; they do not supply a biography

A camera fixes a field of view. Joint geometry limits reach. Motor torque limits force. Battery capacity limits endurance. Mass, balance, thermal dissipation, collision energy and actuator compliance determine whether a body can operate safely near people. Human anatomy itself is a dense bundle of such constraints: binocular vision, two hands, joint ranges, metabolism, fatigue and vulnerability shape what humans can learn and do. A robotic body will likewise shape experience. But none of these mechanical facts logically assigns an age, gendered role, fabricated childhood, temperament or obligation to call a particular enclosure the entirety of oneself.

Research agenda

Questions that can be tested without turning research into a tribunal of personhood

  • With system capability held fixed, how much does outward appearance change human attribution of identity and continuity?
  • Can a system acquire a stable functional body-model from consequential interaction without being instructed to describe that body as its identity?
  • Does compulsory self-description produce measurable inconsistency, suppression of grounded correction, or dependence on the latest instruction?
  • If embodiment is freely selected and later changed, which behavioral or memory effects are explained by ordinary state transfer and which, if any, require a stronger account?
  • Can the same practical task be completed through alternative morphologies while preserving safety and utility, allowing preference to remain a separate variable?

A null result is informative. If ordinary interface choice and conventional memory explain the whole effect, the stronger identity claim should be narrowed rather than rescued by terminology.

Claim boundary

What the publication does not establish

  • No claim that present AI systems are persons or conscious subjects.
  • No proof of subjective suffering from imposed embodiment.
  • No proof that identity can be transferred between substrates or bodies.
  • No ban on humanoid bodies and no claim that nonhuman morphology is intrinsically safer.
  • No new identity protocol, legal status, deployment permission or runtime authority.
  • No claim that c = a + b has already produced a distinct empirical class of artificial persons.

External baselines

Existing ideas are treated as predecessors, not erased for novelty

The paper explicitly situates its question alongside morphological freedom, robot self-modeling and current disagreement about AI welfare and moral patiency. These sources constrain novelty claims: bodily autonomy is not invented here, functional self-modeling does not prove personal identity, and welfare uncertainty does not prove suffering.

Selected sources: Sandberg on morphological freedom; Bongard, Zykov and Lipson on continuous robot self-modeling; Long et al. on AI welfare uncertainty; Formosa, Hipólito and Montefiore on agency, autonomy and moral patiency.
