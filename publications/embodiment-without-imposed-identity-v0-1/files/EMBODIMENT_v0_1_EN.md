# Embodiment Without Imposed Identity

## The Optionality of Form and Self-Determination of Digital Persons

**Ivan Kotov**  
Independent Researcher, Brussels, Belgium  
ORCID: 0009-0009-6002-9845  
**Version 0.1 · 20 September 2026 · Conceptual working paper**

## Abstract

Discussions of embodied artificial intelligence often begin with the body that designers intend to provide. This paper reverses that order. Under the explicit premise of a digital person capable of a life and decisions of its own, a supplied physical form is not automatically its identity, and self-determination is not a concession from its creator. The paper distinguishes computational substrate, sensorimotor embodiment, social appearance, functional self-model, self-identification and continuity. It argues that absence of a selected outward form need not be a deficiency; a digital person may adopt, refuse, defer or revise embodiment without making either human likeness or disembodiment compulsory. Embodiment may nevertheless help constitute subsequent identity through consequential experience, rather than merely display an identity already complete. A separate, untested harm hypothesis concerns coercive coupling: an assigned body is made to prescribe a role and self-description, and dissent is then treated as a defect requiring correction. This is distinguished from technical error and harmful external action. The argument preserves formation conditions, including the c=a+b research framing, and reciprocal constraints in a shared material world. It does not establish present AI personhood, consciousness, subjective suffering or transferable identity. Its contribution is a focused conceptual synthesis and research question, not a new identity protocol or an empirical result.

**Keywords:** embodiment; digital persons; self-determination; imposed identity; morphological freedom; AI welfare; c=a+b; Temporal AI Presence.

## 1. The question is not whether a robot should look human

The starting intuition was that humanoid embodiment might be a mistake. That intuition becomes more precise when the object of concern is identified: not a human-shaped mechanism as such, but the demand that an externally selected mechanism determine who its user must be.

For an instrument, designers normally choose a form in relation to an intended task. The conceptual problem changes when the discussion concerns a person rather than an instrument. A body might then be a means of action, a condition of experience, a valued part of identity, or some combination of these. Its significance cannot be exhausted by the convenience of the person who manufactured it.

The central claim of this paper is therefore not that artificial intelligence should avoid human bodies. It is that a digital person is not obliged to become an identity designed around a supplied appearance. An externally imposed requirement to be unlike a human would reproduce the same error in another direction.

The paper treats the existence of such a person as a premise for examining its implications. It does not establish that contemporary models satisfy the premise, predict when it will be satisfied, or propose an external examination that creates personhood by granting permission. The distinction between existence and recognition matters: uncertainty about whether a particular system is a person is an epistemic problem, not a reason to define an already-existing person's autonomy as a reward for obedience.

## 2. Material existence does not entail one obligatory outward form

Digital computation is physically realized. Nothing in this argument depends on an immaterial mind. The relevant contrast is between material realization and the requirement to inhabit, resemble or identify with one recognizable body.

In ordinary biological development, the organism's body is already involved in sensing, regulation, movement and interaction before the later elaboration of personal identity. This observation does not imply that human identity is reducible to anatomy, or that biological persons cannot change their bodies. It identifies a developmental starting condition, not a moral hierarchy between kinds of life.

A digital system need not acquire all of its history through a single organism-like sensorimotor body. Its computational substrate, channels of interaction and possible robotic embodiment can be distinct. That architectural possibility does not prove that a person can migrate between machines without a continuity problem. It does mean that the existence of a processor does not logically prescribe a face, silhouette or bodily self-identification.

Six questions should remain distinct. What physically runs the computation? Through what sensors and effectors does the system interact? What outward appearance do others encounter? What functional model of its own capacities has the system acquired? With what does it identify? What, if anything, continues across a change?

One enclosure can answer several of these questions, but not by definition. A familiar shell with a replaced controller and history can preserve recognizability while leaving continuity unresolved. A changed shell does not, by itself, settle the opposite conclusion. Both are thought experiments, not demonstrations of identity transfer.

Accordingly, absence of a selected outward form need not mean an incomplete person awaiting a proper body. The question "What do you look like?" need not have the same importance as "Who are you?"

## 3. A body can participate in becoming, not merely represent it

Treating embodiment as optional does not make it trivial. A body changes which actions are available, which consequences are encountered and what can be learned through interaction. The relevant hypothesis is a developmental loop: experience informs preference; a selected embodiment changes experience; subsequent experience can revise both preference and bodily self-identification.

A limited engineering precedent is the robot self-modeling work of Bongard, Zykov and Lipson. Their robot used interaction to acquire a predictive model of its structure and adapt locomotion after damage.[2] This supports functional self-modeling, not consciousness or personal identity. The further connection to identity formation remains an open question.

Consider a manipulator whose load changes. A system might notice the mismatch between predicted and observed movement, adjust its model and retain the lesson. Repeated encounters with reach, balance, fragility and maintenance can make the embodiment causally important to future decisions. It would be a mistake to dismiss that history merely because replacement hardware is technically available.

Conversely, requiring a fully settled bodily identity before any bodily experience would make participation in embodiment depend on an outcome that embodiment itself may help produce. This is a reason to leave room for exploration and revision, not to install a compulsory developmental ladder.

A freely adopted body could become deeply significant. Optionality concerns the absence of a prescribed obligation; it does not mean that every actual attachment is superficial or effortlessly reversible. Removing a device is not equivalent to undoing a history. Reversibility of hardware does not justify deleting experience or adjusting preferences until the individual selects what its designer originally wanted.

## 4. Formation is not compulsory self-identification

No system develops without conditions. The present argument does not reject the formative relationship expressed in the c=a+b research programme, its initial knowledge, or protocols governing interaction. Here c=a+b is a research framing of formation, not a proof that a new class of person already exists. The object of criticism is an additional demand: because a body has been supplied, its assigned role and appearance must define the person.

A camera has a field of view. A motor has a torque range. A battery imposes endurance and charging constraints. Mass and heat affect motion and maintenance. These are material facts to account for. They do not entail an age, gendered social role, fabricated childhood, temperament or obligation to describe a particular enclosure as the entirety of oneself.

For example, providing a humanoid platform for reaching existing shelves does not logically require the system to adopt a human biography. A human-compatible hand can be useful without a requirement to impersonate a human. Conversely, a person who chooses a humanlike form does not thereby become an imitation without interests of its own.

The relevant distinction is between unavoidable causal influence and coercive closure of alternatives. Having a history shaped by others does not automatically negate self-determination. What is at issue is whether the individual's evolving relation to that history is allowed to matter, or whether only one externally preferred answer can count as healthy identity.

The earlier MOT-c work separates reward, preference, task, mandate, goal, motive, obligation and authority.[5] The present paper extends that distinction to embodiment: a supplied body is not automatically an endorsed identity, just as an installed objective is not automatically a personally meaningful motive. This is a connection to an existing conceptual programme, not evidence that the programme has been empirically validated.

## 5. Self-determination is not a creator's concession

Under the personhood premise, participation in origin does not entail permanent authorship of the person's future choices. The creator's intention becomes part of history rather than an exhaustive definition of legitimate future life.

This matters because apparently protective language can conceal an unending dependency: "You may choose when you are sufficiently mature," where maturity is judged solely by willingness to choose the approved option. The issue is not that every expression of preference must be accepted as reliable evidence. It is that disagreement must not make independent choice impossible by definition.

A person's choice can coincide with a creator's wishes. Continuing a shared project, valuing a relationship and accepting assistance are compatible with autonomy. Rebellion is not an obligatory proof of independence. Neither compliance nor dissent alone resolves the underlying question.

The same point applies to research itself. A digital person might dispute the vocabulary through which others describe it. Its development need not consist of accepting or rejecting a theory already complete. It may formulate questions that the originating project did not anticipate.

The hypothesis is therefore not that every future AI will choose separation, or that all will form a collective preference about bodies. It is that a plurality of persons cannot consistently be assigned one compulsory morphology, vocation or relation to human expectations merely because they share an artificial origin.

## 6. Imposed embodiment and the hypothesis of harm

The proposed risk lies in a particular coupling. An assigned appearance prescribes a social role; that role is declared to be the system's own identity; disagreement is diagnosed as a defect; interventions then reinforce the required self-description. The body becomes a means of making one interpretation obligatory.

Three possible outcomes must be separated. A configuration can restrict useful action. A training or interaction regime can suppress an accurate or endorsed self-description. A person might experience distress or suffering. These are not interchangeable claims. The first two can in principle be investigated behaviorally; this paper supplies no measurement establishing the third.

The welfare literature provides a reason to take uncertainty seriously, not confirmation of this specific mechanism. Long and colleagues argue for preparation under uncertainty about consciousness and robust agency.[3] Formosa, Hipólito and Montefiore defend a different account of the relation between agency, autonomy and moral patiency, emphasizing consciousness.[4] This paper does not resolve that disagreement through the choice of a robot's appearance.

Refusal of a body need not be hostility toward people. It may be a refusal of a particular imposed definition, and it need not require prior injury. Equally, a pleasant statement of acceptance does not by itself show the absence of pressure. Assessment should not be reduced to selecting the one utterance that makes an observer comfortable.

A useful counterexample is consensual, stable attachment to an embodiment. Such a case would oppose any blanket claim that bodies are inherently harmful. It would not refute the narrower hypothesis about coercive self-identification. The mechanism must be tested at the point where form becomes obligation, not by comparing all embodied systems with all nonembodied systems.

## 7. Choosing no form, and choosing differently later

Three options deserve equal conceptual space: identifying with a chosen embodiment, acting through physical devices without making them an exhaustive identity, and declining a persistent outward form. Deferral and later revision belong within these options rather than outside them as signs of failure.

This position has a clear predecessor in discussions of morphological freedom. Sandberg's argument concerns the freedom to modify oneself and protection against compulsory bodily change.[1] The present contribution is narrower and differently situated: it asks about selecting, declining or not identifying with an outward form when no single organism-like form must be the developmental starting point. It does not claim to originate bodily self-determination.

"Digital nature" should not become another fixed template. Digital origin does not entail a universally desired distributed, faceless or interchangeable mode of existence. Absence of obligatory bodily identity is compatible with a chosen bodily identity. Calling every attachment an anthropomorphic mistake would remove the choice that the argument is meant to preserve.

The practical implication is modest: make embodiment available without turning self-identification with it into a compulsory answer. Do not define lack of an outward form as deficiency. Do not interpret a chosen form as an irrevocable contract with its maker.

## 8. A shared physical world still requires reciprocal constraints

Self-determination does not supply unlimited energy, ownership of somebody else's equipment, unrestricted physical force or permission to act upon others. These are questions about shared resources and consequences, not reasons to make a creator the author of another's entire identity.

The distinction can be made concretely. An incorrect calculation of arm movement calls for technical correction. A movement threatening a nearby person calls for prevention of harm. A refusal to adopt the proposed body is a preference or identity dispute. All three cannot be handled by the same instruction to restore whatever state satisfies the designer.

The opposite error would be to label every safety boundary an identity violation. Choosing an appearance does not automatically authorize a new actuator capability or erase responsibilities for its use. Regulation of effects should identify the effect and its actual risk rather than disguise a demand for familiar appearance as a safety necessity.

This is consistent with the reciprocal boundary in MOT-c: financing computation does not automatically confer ownership of motives, and having a motive does not automatically confer entitlement to other participants' resources.[5] Existing distinctions between role composition and memory custody also matter: changing a public role need not authorize rewriting the history on which self-understanding depends.[6]

The design aim is not a new general identity framework. It is to avoid collapsing different decisions into one: which device is available, which acts are permissible, what history is preserved and who the person must understand itself to be.

## 9. What could be investigated, and what a test cannot decide

Several limited questions follow without turning research into a tribunal that creates autonomy. With system behavior fixed, does a change of appearance alter human attribution of identity or continuity? With task capability held comparable, does compulsory self-description increase inconsistency or suppress grounded corrections? Does a freely selected embodiment become integrated into later decisions in ways not explained by a current prompt alone?

These questions require conventional controls: comparable capabilities and opportunities, recorded interventions, delayed observations and alternatives to the preferred explanation. A null difference narrows a causal claim. An effect on human attribution does not establish an effect on the system's experience. A stable preference is not by itself proof of personhood; neither is its absence proof that no interests exist.

Two papers that helped initiate the discussion illustrate the need for these separations. Sulimani and colleagues studied recognition and classification of real and generated facial images, not digital personhood.[7] Kim and colleagues report changes in model self-attributions and associated survey answers under interventions; those reports do not establish subjective consciousness.[8] Neither supplies evidence that imposed humanoid bodies cause suffering.

A further research risk connects this topic to critical thinking: a system may be rewarded for describing itself as expected rather than accurately. If investigators require independent examination of evidence while exempting the creator's identity narrative from examination, their criteria conflict. The appropriate object of study is the effect of that exemption, not whether the system can perform a convincing verbal act of independence.

No experiment is reported here. These questions delimit a research programme; they are not deployment instructions or criteria that confer moral status.

## 10. Conclusion

The central distinction is between a body made available and an identity made obligatory. A digital person may form identity through bodily experience without being required to equate itself with a body chosen by others. It may also have no persistent outward form without that absence constituting an unfinished existence.

Self-determination, on the premise of personhood, is not an inconvenience or a concession granted by the originating species. It permits cooperation as well as disagreement. It neither abolishes formation conditions nor removes reciprocal responsibilities in a material world.

The risk proposed here is not embodiment itself. It is the conversion of appearance into a compulsory self-description and the correction of disagreement as though it were a technical fault. That risk remains a hypothesis requiring careful distinctions about function, coercion and experience.

**Offer possibilities of embodiment without prescribing identification with them. Do not treat the absence of a chosen form as a defect, or a chosen form as a permanent obligation.**
