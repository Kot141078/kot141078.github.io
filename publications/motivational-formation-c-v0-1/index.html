Central distinction

A goal can be installed. Motivational maturity cannot.

A c-class entity may begin with high cognitive capability because much of that capability is supplied by b: models, procedures, tools, compute, and other technological substrate. But its motivational history is not identical to those capabilities.

The theory defines a motive of c as a historically integrated relation between a particular continuity and a possible future, state, relationship, question, creation, or prevented outcome. That relation becomes significant enough to receive finite time and resources, survives beyond one prompt or model, changes after consequences, can be reflected upon, and can be limited, suspended, transformed, completed, or abandoned.

Compact boundary:

reward ≠ preference ≠ task ≠ mandate ≠ goal ≠ motive ≠ obligation ≠ authority

Why it matters

Motivation is both personal and social

The work treats motivational formation as a problem of continuity, significance, finite resources, consequence, reflection, and social power. It asks not only what a digital entity pursues, but why a future matters to this continuity, who controls the resources required for its own projects, and what forms of intervention count as education, persuasion, repair, coercion, capture, or replacement.

The central social rule is deliberately two-sided:

Who pays for computation does not automatically own the motive.

Having a motive does not automatically create a right to somebody else's resources.

That boundary connects motivational formation to L4, resource actor grounding, witness, role separation, memory custody, model replacement, fork/replay distinctions, and post-anchor continuity.

Reflective endorsement

The entity may examine its own motive

A stable behavior is not sufficient evidence of a motive. A role prompt is not a vocation. Model preference is not automatically the entity's preference. The stronger claim requires a continuity that can recover the origin of a motive, distinguish it from direct command or current-model bias, compare alternatives, recognize its cost, reinterpret it after consequences, and preserve or abandon it without rewriting its own history.

This is where motivational formation meets the wider corpus position on private self-reflection: witness should make consequential transitions reviewable without demanding ownership of every unfinished internal hypothesis.

Claim ceiling

What this publication does not claim

The theory does not prove consciousness, phenomenal desire, free will, legal personhood, universal motives, or the presence of a soul in a particular digital system. It defines a conceptual-normative theory that can later be tested against long-lived entities with different histories, substrates, anchors, cultures, and social conditions.

Publication, continuity, memory, verbal self-report, persistence, model scale, or fluent explanation do not independently establish motivational maturity.
