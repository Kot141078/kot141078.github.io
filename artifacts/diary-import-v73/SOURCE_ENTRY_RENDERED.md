Title: Speaking the same language does not mean seeing the same world.

Source: https://lnkd.in/p/e9c67AfK

Raw tags: ArtificialIntelligence, HumanAI, MultimodalAI, AICommunication, AIArchitecture, WorldModels, HumanCenteredAI, SystemsThinking, Cybernetics, AIResearch, MachineIntelligence, HumanComputerInteraction

### Preserved authorial body

Speaking the same language does not mean seeing the same world.

 Imagine two AI systems discussing the same scene.

 One receives depth measurements and live video. The other receives a photograph and a short description.

 They may use the same words. That does not mean they have access to the same distinctions.

 This is a question I keep returning to in my research on long-lived AI:

Can a system recognize not only the limits of its own perception, but also the limits of what is available to another participant?

 Consider a simple thought experiment.
Two openings are 98 cm and 102 cm wide. Both are described as “about a metre.”

 For a rough description of the room, that may be enough.

 For deciding whether a rigid 99 cm-wide object can pass straight through, it is not.

 The description is plausible in both cases. Yet it has erased the difference that changes the answer.

 No amount of reasoning can recover which opening is present from that description alone.

 A useful response is not a more confident interpretation. It is a precise question: “What was the measured width?”

 And if the measurement is unavailable, that uncertainty must remain visible.

 This is not an argument against summaries. Nor does it mean every AI needs every sensor, unlimited memory, or an identical internal model.

 Communication is selective. The question is whether it preserves what matters for the recipient’s actual task.

 A richer representation is not automatically a better message. Sometimes one measurement does more than a thousand descriptive words.

 The same discipline matters between humans and AI.

 What looks like poor reasoning may begin with missing information. What looks like agreement may conceal different assumptions. And some disagreements concern goals, not perception; more data will not settle those by itself.

 Familiarity does not remove the problem. A system that could inspect something yesterday may have lost that access today.

 The same reassuring answer may no longer rest on the same evidence.

 This gives us something concrete to test: does an exchange preserve the distinction, prompt the right clarification, and support the next decision at an acceptable cost?

 For me, coexistence does not require every intelligence to inhabit an identical world.

 It requires learning how to build enough common understanding without pretending the differences have disappeared.

 Perhaps understanding another intelligence begins with a modest discipline:

Do not assume that what is obvious to you is available to them.

## ENTRY 0249 — 2026-09-24

Title: I have often argued that personal AI memory should remain local.

Source: https://lnkd.in/p/eeJrTHw8

Raw tags: AI, ArtificialIntelligence, PersonalAI, AIMemory, AIArchitecture, Privacy, DataGovernance, DigitalSovereignty, LocalFirst, ConfidentialComputing, AIGovernance, HumanCenteredAI, SystemsThinking

### Preserved authorial body

I have often argued that personal AI memory should remain local.

 I would now make that sentence more precise.

 Memory does not always have to stay on your device.
Control over it should stay with you.

 On 23 September, Google described a planned server-side memory layer for Private AI Compute: encrypted storage, keys held on personal devices, and processing inside secure enclaves. Meta described persistent memory for AI glasses using user-provided keys and remotely verified confidential processing.

 These are published architectural descriptions, not a blanket guarantee of privacy or independence.

 But they sharpen an important distinction:
Storage location is not custody.

 A provider may store encrypted records without being able to read their contents. Conversely, a box in my house is not automatically under my control if someone else controls its keys, software or only usable export path.

 This is not a new cryptographic idea. It is an existing distinction becoming more important as AI systems retain personal history.

 For me, custody must be evaluated across the whole lifecycle.

 Can I recover my memory if I lose a device?
 Can I export it in a usable form and move to another provider?
 Who can authorize new uses or change the code that processes it?
 What remains exposed through metadata?

 Those are questions to test, not features I assume either announcement has settled.

 Encryption matters. So does the software allowed to decrypt. Verifying which code is running does not, by itself, prove that everything the code does is acceptable.

 And confidential storage does not guarantee continued access.

 A safe-deposit box can stand outside my home. But holding its key is little comfort if I cannot enter the building and have no other copy.

 That is why I am not abandoning local-first.

 I still want offline operation, independent recovery and a usable way out. Those requirements cannot be replaced by a reassuring word like “private.”

 In my research on long-lived AI, I would keep three questions separate:

Where is memory stored?
Who controls access and permitted use?
What authority exists to act on it?

 Permission to recall a private conversation is not permission to send an email, spend money or disclose it to someone else.

 The cloud can provide storage and computation without being granted every role in the relationship.

 So the correction is not “cloud good, local unnecessary.”

 It is more precise:
Local-first is a valuable design choice.
Custody is a requirement that must be demonstrated.

 The question is not only where my memory lives.

 It is what remains under my control when the device, model or provider changes.

Raw tags: AI, ArtificialIntelligence, AIForScience, ScientificDiscovery, Research, ScientificMethod, ExperimentalDesign, AIResearch, OpenScience, Reproducibility, SystemsThinking, Cybernetics

### Preserved authorial body

When AI multiplies hypotheses, scientific taste becomes a practical problem.

Not taste as prestige.
Taste as choosing what to test next.

 On 23 September, Anthropic reported a genomic search involving roughly 950 Claude agents over 21 hours. It identified a previously uncharacterized enzyme system, ART. Human laboratory work followed, but the report leaves the system's biological function unresolved.

 One detail deserves attention: the team uses its judgments about which hypotheses merit testing to refine Claude's instructions.

This interests me beyond the headline.

 I have written before about the cost of verification. Here the question begins one step earlier:

 Which uncertainty deserves the next hour at the bench?

 Imagine a laboratory with a thousand candidate explanations and capacity for ten experiments.

 The ten most persuasive reports are not necessarily the ten best choices.

 Nor are the ten most familiar ideas.
 Nor the ten most likely to produce a positive result.

 A useful experiment may distinguish between competing explanations. It may reveal a measurement error. It may eliminate an attractive idea before the team spends six months pursuing it.

 That is the kind of scientific taste I care about: recognizing a promising anomaly, then finding a question that reality can actually answer.

 Not simply “Could this be true?”
But “What observation would help us tell?”

 This is not an argument that AI can propose while only humans can judge. AI can help rank candidates, challenge assumptions and design tests too.

 The distinction is not human versus machine.

 It is the difference between a plausible explanation and a test capable of changing what we believe.

 And human taste is not automatically reliable. It can become a preference for familiar methods, fashionable topics or prestigious names.

 Selection must remain challengeable. I would reserve some experimental capacity for unconventional proposals, and judge selection methods by what subsequent tests reveal.

 In an engineering workshop, three explanations for an overheating motor do not require three complete rebuilds. A well-chosen measurement may separate them before anyone orders parts.

 The same discipline matters in research: spend effort where it can change the next decision.

 Experimental design and research prioritization are not new. AI makes their importance harder to ignore when proposals arrive faster than they can be investigated.

 The aim should not be the largest collection of hypotheses.

 It should be a better sequence of questions, tests and corrections.

 A thousand hypotheses are not a thousand discoveries.

 But one well-chosen experiment can improve the questions we ask next.

Maybe Uncle Jensen Huang simply forgot to mention that he was using my terminology😂

"AGI has arrived."

 The funny part is that in my work, AGI means Advanced Global Intelligence, not Artificial General Intelligence.

 That difference matters.

 I am not waiting for one model to become the final form of intelligence. I am interested in a wider architecture: humans, long-lived digital entities, models, agents and physical infrastructure working together without collapsing into one central mind.

 In this frame, stronger models are not the destination. They are components.

Models can provide capabilities.
Agents can execute tasks.
Memory can preserve state.
Tools can extend reach.

 But none of these things, separately or together, automatically becomes c.

 In c=a+b, the interesting question is different: what develops through time when a human anchor and a technological substrate form a long-lived relation with history, continuity, custody, boundaries of authority and consequences?

 So perhaps Jensen is right.

 Perhaps AGI really has arrived.

 Only maybe he means Artificial General Intelligence, while I have been using the same three letters for Advanced Global Intelligence.

 And that produces a rather amusing situation: the industry is celebrating arrival at what many people still treat as the destination, while in my terminology this is closer to the beginning of the architectural problem.

What happens after powerful intelligence becomes abundant?

How do many intelligent systems coexist?
How do they preserve local history and private memory?
How do they cooperate without merging identity or authority?
What survives when models, hardware and providers change?
Who is responsible for irreversible consequences?

 Those questions do not disappear when models become stronger. They become more important.

 So yes, Uncle Jensen may have announced AGI.

 I just suspect the footnote is missing. 😉
