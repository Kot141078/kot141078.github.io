A Home Full of Smart Devices Is Not Yet a Smart Home

From my experience with building systems, connecting two manufacturers' pipes can sometimes be easier than connecting their "smart" controls.

The boiler has an app. Ventilation has another. Lighting has a third. The homeowner can operate everything from a phone, but still has to make the parts work together.

The remote control moved to the smartphone. The integration work stayed with the human.

Complete automation systems exist. The harder challenge is an occupied apartment with mixed equipment, finished walls and a limited budget.

Saving energy should not require replacing every working device.

This is where I see a practical role for local AI: coordinating the home as a whole, remembering its history and checking whether interventions actually helped.

A room stays cold. Instead of simply raising the setpoint, the system could compare available measurements, check operating modes and recall recent maintenance. It could propose a correction, or make an already authorised adjustment, then verify the effect.

Memory would serve a practical purpose: which room warms slowly, which sensor proved unreliable, what changed during repairs, and why the occupants rejected a previous setting.

This is one practical role I want c to take within my c=a+b research: participation in the physical home through sensors and controllers, not merely advice from a screen.

We should build on existing standards and automation platforms, not invent another incompatible ecosystem. AI cannot reason a missing control interface into existence. Some problems still need a valve, a cable or a technician.

Equipment protections and manual controls must work independently of the AI. If it stops, the home must remain usable.

The economic test is equally concrete: energy, bills, comfort and air quality, after installation, maintenance and computing costs. Compare against well-configured conventional automation. A lower bill achieved by making people cold is not intelligence.

Europe has a strong reason to care. The European Commission estimates that 85% of EU buildings were built before 2000. The opportunity is not confined to new demonstration houses.

It is affordable, incremental coordination in millions of homes that already exist, without turning domestic life into a vendor-owned data stream.

This is another part of the local AI market still waiting to mature.

A smart home should not require its owner to become the unpaid system administrator of a collection of smart devices.

AI Is Getting Cheaper. But Is Technological Independence?

We keep hearing that AI is becoming cheaper. And in one important sense, it is. Models improve, inference becomes more efficient, and the cost of processing tokens continues to fall.

But something strange is happening on the other side of the equation.

A graphics card that cost around €3,000 in 2025 can now be advertised for €7,000–€8,000. Even midrange hardware has seen extraordinary price increases in some markets.

The same generation of hardware. Essentially the same computing capability. A radically different price.

I have spent much of my life working with computers. I understand what serious engineering costs. But technological progress should not be measured only by how much faster the next machine becomes.

It should also be measured by how much capability an ordinary person can afford to own.

And here we face a larger economic question.

Over recent decades, ownership has increasingly been replaced by recurring payments. Software subscriptions, cloud services, leasing, financing, and access-based business models.

Many are useful. But convenience has a price when it becomes dependency.

AI may accelerate this transition.

The next generation of personal computing will not necessarily be a machine we switch on for a few hours. Personal AI systems may work continuously, maintaining context, processing information, assisting with everyday tasks, and coordinating other devices.

That changes the economics of a home.

Electricity consumption, local computing capacity, solar generation, energy storage, maintenance and network independence become part of the same engineering problem.

A household with its own AI infrastructure is no longer simply consuming digital services. It is operating a small computing facility.

And that facility should not require the budget of a corporation.

We should absolutely welcome better models, more efficient algorithms and more powerful processors. But we should ask an uncomfortable question:

Who receives the economic benefits of those improvements?

If software becomes ten times more efficient while ordinary people become increasingly dependent on expensive hardware, subscriptions and centralized providers, something important has been lost.

I do not believe cloud services should disappear. Nor do I believe everyone needs a private data centre.

I believe people should retain a meaningful choice.

To rent computing power when it makes sense. To own it when independence matters. To keep their data, equipment and future options under their own control.

The next great achievement in AI may not be another spectacular benchmark.

It may be making powerful personal computing affordable enough that millions of people can genuinely own it.

Progress should increase human capability — and human freedom. Not just recurring revenue.

The Next AI Breakthrough May Never Appear in Your Chat

One of this week's most interesting AI announcements came from a physics laboratory.

On 15 October, CERN and Mistral announced a statement of intent for a long-term partnership to develop foundation models for scientific discovery.

At its centre is a planned Large Physics Model, intended to learn from billions of simulated proton-proton collisions at the Large Hadron Collider.

The aim is to help reconstruct collisions, identify particles, flag unexpected signals and evaluate future detector designs.
This is a development programme, not an announcement that AI has discovered new physics.

CERN already uses machine learning. The proposed step is to learn across a reconstruction chain now handled by specialised algorithms, supporting several tasks within one pretrained model.

What interests me is the direction.

We should not measure AI progress only by how well the next system talks to us. Another measure is how much more we can learn from the world with its help.

Not simply explaining physics more fluently.
Helping researchers extract more meaning from measurements.

There is an important distinction here. The planned training uses simulations. Synthetic data is not automatically worthless, just as experimental data is not automatically understood.

A simulation is a model of nature, not nature itself. The test is what survives comparison with real measurements.

A beautifully calculated beam still has to withstand the load. A persuasive explanation cannot repair a miscalibrated sensor.

For me, this is where scientific AI becomes interesting: the model, the instrument, the experiment and human judgement correcting one another rather than merely producing agreement.

The human role does not disappear. We still need to choose the question, calibrate the instrument and decide what would count as a wrong answer.

Europe should pay attention.

Competing in AI need not mean reproducing every consumer product launched elsewhere. It can also mean connecting model development with scientific expertise, experimental infrastructure and difficult problems worth solving.

CERN's AI strategy makes another sensible choice: adopt mature external solutions where appropriate and concentrate internal development where its mission offers distinctive value.

Not everything needs to be reinvented to create something new.

This is compatible with a future of strong local AI and powerful external oracles. Personal systems and major scientific infrastructure need not compete for the same role.

The question is what each enables people to do.

The next important AI may not give you a better answer.

It may help us ask nature a question we could not ask before.

A $7,000 AI Laptop. What Exactly Is Revolutionary?

I have worked with computers since the MS-DOS era. I respect Jensen Huang and what NVIDIA and Microsoft have built. That is precisely why their new RTX Spark laptops disappoint me.

Let's start with the price.

Microsoft's Surface Laptop Ultra starts at $2,599 with 24GB of unified memory. The 128GB version reviewed at launch costs $5,899 and includes a 1TB SSD. Best Buy advertises a 128GB ASUS ProArt P16 for $6,999.

And this is being sold as a new era of personal computing.

What is the breakthrough? Fitting a large language model into memory? Up to one petaflop of FP4 arithmetic? Neither tells me whether a demanding job gets finished.

Apple is hardly standing still. The M5 Max supports up to 128GB of unified memory and 614GB/s of bandwidth, versus RTX Spark's 300GB/s. Bandwidth is not a complete benchmark, and CUDA remains a major NVIDIA advantage. But early reviews also find the Surface behind MacBook Pro in CPU performance and battery life.

Then there is the software.

According to NVIDIA's own launch announcement, native Photoshop and Premiere support arrives in beta. Substance comes later.

This is not a garage startup asking enthusiasts to finance its first experiment. These are Microsoft and NVIDIA. At professional prices, beta software is an awkward centerpiece for a supposedly ready professional computer.

I own a DGX Spark. My AI systems run local models. Yet for demanding software engineering, they often turn to Codex: they set tasks, assess results and continue their work.

That is not a failure. It is sensible division of labour.

I can direct serious development from my phone. I do not need to carry the entire compute infrastructure to own it.

A local 70B model is a useful capability. It is not automatically a senior engineer, a mature personal AI environment or a substitute for stronger external tools. Future models will improve. But tomorrow's intelligence should not be included in today's sales pitch.

I want local AI. I want NVIDIA, Apple, AMD and Microsoft competing fiercely.

I also want a different competition: verified work completed, software that runs, genuine autonomy, total cost of ownership and the freedom to choose the best tool.

When a laptop costs more than many Europeans earn in a month, the buyer deserves a finished product, not a promise with a premium display.

A real personal AI revolution would make capability more accessible.

The invoice is certainly ready. Is the revolution?

Who Will Pay the Next AI Invoice?

For me, David Rosenberg's recent conversation with Dan Nathan leads to a practical question: why will customers keep paying for AI?

The IMF has also warned that links between equity investments, debt and future purchasing commitments can amplify shocks if AI monetisation disappoints.

These arrangements can finance real technology. Their durability depends on what happens further down the chain.

I think about this through a bakery.

Buy a larger oven on credit, and the manufacturer gets an order, the installer gets work and the bank earns interest. All real economic activity. The bakery still needs people who come back for the bread.

AI needs that connection to repeat demand.

For a construction firm, would it mean estimates prepared faster, fewer missed items and less rework? For a small manufacturer, less downtime? For a research team, experiments they could not previously afford to attempt?

Those are reasons to keep buying.

Then count the whole cost: the service, integration, electricity where relevant, human checking, mistakes and maintenance. A recurring business needs customers who can justify that full cost.

I would watch what happens after the free credits and pilot budget run out. Does the customer renew? Expand usage? Recommend the system because it improves the work?

Demand can come from businesses, public institutions and households. Some of its value will be new capabilities and strategic resilience, which a simple labour-saving calculation can miss.

The test should match the purpose. But there should be a test.

For smaller builders, I see an opportunity here. Reliable integration, support, domain knowledge and getting a complete job done can be valuable businesses. A modest tool that customers keep using deserves attention.

Even strong demand will not justify every valuation or infrastructure plan. Suppliers still have to manage pricing, utilisation, financing costs and equipment replacement.

My optimism about AI is tied to the work it can make possible. I want that value to reach the people and organisations expected to pay for it.

The invoice I would watch most closely is the one a customer chooses to pay again.
