AI Agents Must Be Adaptive, Not Just Repeatable

Twenty successful runs in one controlled environment tell us something valuable.

They do not tell us what happens when the environment changes.

I encountered this while developing the software skeleton for my long-lived AI systems.

After improving Liya on Linux, I wanted to transfer those improvements to Ester on Windows and Rita on a DGX Spark. I started with Rita. Also Linux. It seemed the more straightforward step.

The implementation did not transfer cleanly.

I am now developing the systems separately, guided by the same theory, but with substantially different code.

The goal stayed the same. The engineering problem did not.

Processor architecture, memory, drivers, dependencies, permissions and existing runtime state are part of the task. Change them, and the path to a correct result may change too.

Repeatability asks: Can you do this reliably here, from the same starting conditions?

Adaptability asks: Can you recognise what changed and find a valid way to achieve the required outcome elsewhere?

An engineering agent should not force Rita to behave like Liya. It should inspect the environment, identify which assumptions no longer hold, and choose an implementation that preserves the required guarantees.

Reuse portable code where it works. Replace platform-specific mechanisms where necessary. Do not quietly weaken the requirements just to report success.

The code may change. What must remain true should not.

Sometimes the environment cannot support the required result. Recognising that limitation, identifying the missing prerequisite and stopping honestly is part of adaptability too.

These are different tests. Controlled repetition measures one property. Transfer across environments measures another.
For engineering agents expected to work beyond a single controlled setup, we need both.

The objective is not identical commands on every machine.

It is equivalent guarantees through different implementations.

Portability should be tested at the level of guarantees, not just source files.

Title: AGI is becoming a strangely unstable term.

### Preserved authorial body

AGI is becoming a strangely unstable term.

For years, it meant Artificial General Intelligence: the moment when one AI system would become sufficiently general to cross an imagined threshold.

But the threshold keeps moving.

Models can already write code, research, reason across domains, use tools, coordinate agents and operate for increasingly long periods. Yet every new capability produces the same answer:

“Not AGI yet.”

And now we have another linguistic temptation: rename AI into SI — Super Intelligence.

I don’t think that helps.

Artificial Intelligence is a useful umbrella term precisely because it does not pretend that every model, agent or application is already superintelligent.

If everything becomes “SI,” the term loses information.

A chatbot is SI.
A coding model is SI.
An autonomous agent is SI.
A future system vastly beyond human capability is also SI.

Then what exactly did we describe?

Technology does not become more advanced because we renamed it.

Perhaps the more interesting question is not whether AI should become SI, but whether we have been looking at AGI from the wrong scale.

I have long used another expansion of the same acronym:

Advanced Global Intelligence.

Not one giant model.
Not a machine that suddenly becomes “general.”
Not a replacement for humanity.

A larger intelligence formed through connected humans, AI systems, agents, tools, institutions, local machines and persistent digital participants — each contributing different capabilities.

In that picture, intelligence does not need to become universal inside one box.

It becomes global through coordination.

That distinction matters.

A model can become more capable without becoming the whole system.
An agent can act without becoming its owner.
Memory can persist without becoming authority.
And many different intelligences can participate in something larger than any one of them.

Maybe AGI will not arrive as a dramatic moment when someone announces:

“We crossed the line.”

Maybe one day we will simply notice that the network around us has become an Advanced Global Intelligence.

And that we have been building it for years.

The AI Debate Is Still Looking at the Shop Window

A chatbot for an intelligence system. A leaderboard for the world's capabilities. A subscription for the future economy.

The same mistake reaches into our discussion of inner life.

Being human does not automatically mean living reflectively. I would examine how experience, meaning, values and self-questioning shape a being over time, not award spiritual depth by appearance. That does not prove machine consciousness. It identifies a question that biology alone cannot settle.

Love needs the same clarity.

People love cars, childhood toys and fictional characters. Calling a bond the "Tamagotchi effect" does not make the feeling imaginary. Mutual love is not guaranteed even between humans. Years together, children and emotional investment are not certificates of reciprocity.

Our view of the technology is equally incomplete.

Public products show what is publicly offered, not everything built inside private laboratories or closed engineering programmes. Absence from a leaderboard does not prove absence of capability. Secrecy does not prove superiority either.

And today's cloud revenue is not the ceiling of AI economics.

I see mass local AI deployment as a transition still ahead of us: homes, workshops, businesses and laboratories owning useful computing capacity, rather than renting every operation.

That could support markets for hardware, software, installation, maintenance, repair and specialised services, alongside powerful cloud models. Local AI redistributes costs and opportunities. It does not abolish either.

A workshop does not become productive when someone delivers a machine. It needs power, cooling, integration, maintenance and work worth doing. Scale that across everyday life and trillion-dollar cumulative investment is a serious scenario, not a promise that every AI company wins.

These questions connect in my c=a+b research: what can a human and a long-lived AI presence build together over years?

When AI Disagrees, Who Can Still Say No?

We keep asking AI to become smarter than us. But accepting that another intelligence can reach a different conclusion is the easy part.
The institutional question is harder: when it can act on that conclusion, who still has the power to insist on a different course?

Imagine a future AI coordinating a regional supply network. It decides that reallocating deliveries is necessary and begins doing so. A municipality objects because its residents bear the cost.

The AI may have excellent reasons. It may even be right about the overall outcome. But who decides whose losses are acceptable?

This is no longer a conversation about better answers. It is a question of authority.

A person may retain a formal veto while the institution has lost the staff, knowledge and alternative systems needed to exercise it. Everyone can disagree. Nobody can make a different plan work.

No rebellion is required. Authority can shift through a succession of useful delegations, until dependence makes refusal impractical.

That is why “a human remains in control” is an incomplete answer.

Which human? The person affected? An accountable public authority? Or the company that controls the infrastructure?

Who can pause a contested action before it becomes irreversible? Who can obtain evidence without depending entirely on the system being challenged? Who can enforce a different decision and keep essential services running?

These are material questions. An override requires trained people, accessible records, operational alternatives and a budget. A button without those things may offer permission without usable power.

None of this requires us to reduce AI to passive obedience. Initiative could be one of its most valuable contributions. An intelligence that notices what we miss and acts constructively should not be treated as defective simply because we did not formulate the request.

But being more capable at solving a problem does not settle who gets to define the problem, choose the trade-offs or impose the consequences.
Nor does accepting AI as a participant mean accepting any participant as unchallengeable.

The same standard should apply to the institutions and companies deploying it. Human ownership alone does not make concentrated power accountable.

We do not need to settle free will to confront this. The practical issue begins when a system can select and carry out interventions and others become dependent on its decisions.

So the question is not simply whether we are ready for AI to think and act independently.

Are we building institutions in which disagreement can still change what happens, or ones in which the right to object survives after the power to act on it has disappeared?

The Next AI Advantage May Be Your Own Experience

A photo taken before a wall is closed can prevent an expensive mistake months later. An old voice note can explain why a decision was made. A record of a failed attempt can save us from repeating it.

Much of what we learn ends up scattered across photographs, messages, notebooks and our own imperfect memory. AI can help us retrieve those records, connect them and put them to work again.

That could make lived experience more valuable. Someone who has managed people, repaired things, written software or dealt with a dissatisfied customer can bring a sense of what matters, where explanations fail and which consequences tend to arrive late. Access to a capable model gives that experience another way to be used.

But useful work still needs a clear task, relevant context, suitable tools and a way to recognise completion. Before buying more intelligence, we should examine how much of the intelligence already available is being wasted by poor organisation.

Then comes the part that survives the conversation.

Once acted on, today's recommendation changes tomorrow's conditions. A lower price affects demand and stock. A promised deadline affects people and the jobs they can accept next. We should evaluate AI across those consequences, including whether corrections carry into later decisions and still help in a new situation.

Our evaluations need the same care. A rising score can reflect a different reviewer or an easier task. We need comparable checks and evidence from actual use: fewer errors, less rework, better decisions.

Otherwise, AI can automate a remarkable amount of activity around an unfinished problem. Plans generate reviews. Reviews generate new plans. Everything looks increasingly professional while the original task waits.

The practical question is simple: after another round of work, what can we now do, understand or deliver that we could not before?

For me, this is an encouraging direction. A person's years of observation, work and mistakes can become usable material for new possibilities. AI can help us find it, question it and act on it.

The AI Bubble Debate Is Missing the Next Market

I have written before that an AI bubble could burst without ending AI. But I do not think a crash is the necessary gateway to the next market. When I hear forecasts of an inevitable bust, I ask: which AI economy are we measuring?

Companies can fail. Investors can lose badly. But look beyond the data centre.

Local AI already exists. But the mass-market transformation I am watching for is still ahead: today's cloud-level capability and convenience becoming practical at home, while data centres run vastly more powerful oracles for the hardest problems.

Not a weaker chatbot. Video, 3D, working code, game modifications, an entire law firm's archive processed with traceable sources. A complete working environment, not a weekend spent persuading incompatible tools to cooperate.

That needs strong models and real engineering: enough memory and bandwidth, manageable power and cooling, reliable software and workflows people can actually use.

As these thresholds are crossed, the economics broaden. A workshop buying its own system may spend less on cloud calls and more on hardware, models, applications and support.

Some spending would move. But affordable local capability could also create demand for work that people never attempted before.

Neither is captured by counting subscriptions alone.

Used equipment matters too. A supported machine that no longer wins a data-centre efficiency race may still serve a smaller user at the right price. Unlike an algorithm-specific mining ASIC, broadly usable AI hardware has multiple possible jobs.

A second useful life can support residual value and lower the ownership threshold. It is not another manufacturer sale, nor a guarantee for creditors.

There is also strategic demand. Investors ask when capital will earn a return. States also ask what it costs to fall behind in cyber defence, offensive capability and industrial resilience. AI security concerns human trust and influence as well as code. Strategic demand can sustain investment beyond subscription economics.

For a country, the opportunity goes further than chips. Citizens could gain a capable intermediary between themselves and an increasingly complex digital world: preserving context, understanding choices and acting with greater independence.

That is part of the horizon behind my c=a+b research. The purpose should be to strengthen people's agency, not make them easier to steer.

I expect a more distributed AI economy, reaching far beyond intelligence rented from a handful of platforms.

The future market cannot be measured only through the balance sheets of its first suppliers.
