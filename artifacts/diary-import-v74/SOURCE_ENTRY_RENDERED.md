Title: For years, the “personal computer” was becoming less personal.

Canonical: https://ivankotov.eu/diary/for-years-the-personal-computer-was-becoming-less-personal/

LinkedIn: https://lnkd.in/p/eWttTKKT

Raw tags (10): AI, PersonalAI, LocalAI, EdgeAI, AgenticAI, FutureOfComputing, HumanComputerInteraction, DigitalInfrastructure, AIHardware, TechPhilosophy

### Preserved body

For years, the “personal computer” was becoming less personal.

It was turning into a polished window into other people’s systems: their clouds, their platforms, their subscriptions, their interfaces.

AI may reverse that.

Not because the cloud disappears. It won’t.

But because local compute is becoming strong enough for something more interesting: not just running software, but hosting a persistent personal intelligence environment.

And that changes the role of applications.

I do not think apps will vanish tomorrow. But I do think they may begin a quiet decline as the primary interface.

For a long time, we opened apps to reach functions. Now we are moving toward systems where intelligence chooses the function, the tool, and the sequence for us.

The app does not necessarily disappear. It just stops being the main character.

The next interface may not be another app. It may be the intelligence that knows which app to use, when to use it, and when not to bother you with it at all.

That is why local AI hardware matters.

A powerful personal machine at home or at work is not just “more performance”. It is the difference between renting intelligence by the request and giving it an actual place to reside.

The cloud gave AI access. Personal compute may give it an address.

And once AI has an address, the computer starts becoming personal again in a deeper sense: not merely a screen, but part of a lived environment shared across desk, phone, wearable devices, and remote compute.

We may soon look back and realize: the most important app on the computer was the one that made the rest of the apps less important.

Title: The next AI price increase may sell me a workstation, not a bigger subscription.

### Preserved body

The next AI price increase may sell me a workstation, not a bigger subscription.

OpenAI now offers Pro 500 at $500/month. Eligible existing Pro 200 subscribers keep their previous included allowance through October 29, 2026, then move to a lower allowance at the same $200 price.

For heavy users, the question is not just the price on the invoice. It is how much real work that invoice pays for.

I already pay for two Pro subscriptions. My usage did not explode while writing theory. It exploded when I moved into custom code. I knew that bringing the design to life would be the hard part.

Not "make me an app."

Make this particular architecture work as intended. Preserve its boundaries. Handle interruption, conflicting state and recovery without quietly changing what the system means.

I see very capable models working hard on these tasks. The difficulty is real.

A drawing is not a sculpture. Turning a design into something that actually works is another kind of creative work, with its own resistance and discipline.

Change recovery logic, and you must examine pending writes, permissions and old data after an interruption. A patch that looks elegant but breaks one of these is not elegant.

This is craft. Sometimes it takes thirty lines. Sometimes it means removing a mechanism that looked clever yesterday.

More code is not the objective. Neither is complexity.

The skill is not merely asking the tool to cut. It is knowing which cut preserves the object you intended to make.

That is why I am looking harder at expanding local compute: DGX Spark-class machines and larger workstations. Not trophies. Equipment for a workshop with actual work waiting.

I expect some work to become slower and require more iteration. I would still use frontier cloud models where their extra capability justifies the cost. More retries are not a guarantee of correctness.

Local is not free: hardware, electricity, depreciation, maintenance and my own time all belong in the calculation.

But buying useful compute gives me a different cost structure. I want the option to keep working without every difficult iteration becoming another credit-purchase decision.

My measure is cost per accepted, verified change. Not tokens per second or subscription prestige.

And $500 a month is $6,000 a year. Being a professional does not make that money imaginary. Independent development can slow down simply because the tools consume too much of its budget.

Cloud AI remains valuable. But making it more expensive for people who actually build may strengthen the case for owning more of the workshop.

I am not trying to generate more code.

I am trying to finish what I actually set out to build.

Title: When people say AI will destroy "laptop jobs", I think the question is framed incorrectly.

LinkedIn: https://lnkd.in/p/e6DVJ_36

Raw tags (7): ArtificialIntelligence, FutureOfWork, Automation, Productivity, LocalAI, AIInfrastructure, HumanAI

### Preserved body

When people say AI will destroy "laptop jobs", I think the question is framed incorrectly.

The laptop is just an interface.

What may disappear is a huge layer of work where the human was effectively the interface between different pieces of software.

Open Excel.
Copy data from one system to another.
Compare tables.
Prepare a report.
Move numbers into a presentation.
Write a standard email.
Find a document.
Fill in a form.

Typing fast was once a profession.

Then typists largely disappeared.

But we did not stop writing.

Something similar may happen with Excel, CRM, SAP, browsers and dozens of office applications.

Excel does not need to disappear.

The human simply may no longer need to press every button personally.

Today:

human -> applications -> data -> result

Increasingly tomorrow:

human -> goal, constraints and responsibility -> AI -> applications, data and tools -> result

So the more interesting question is not:

"Will laptop jobs survive?"

It is:

"How much more will one person be able to do through a laptop?"

Today someone may prepare a report for a department.

Tomorrow the same person may ask:

"Why is our margin falling? Check purchasing, logistics, working hours, seasonality and planning errors. Show me three possible responses, their costs and their risks."

That is still a person sitting at a computer.

But it is not the same job.

The same applies outside the office.

On a construction site, AI can calculate materials, inspect estimates, compare photographs with plans, follow deliveries and flag deviations.

But somebody still has to understand why the wall is there, whether drilling in that place is safe, whether the work should be redone, who takes responsibility and which risk is acceptable.

The machine becomes more capable.

The useful human task moves higher.

So I would not say:

"AI will take laptop jobs."

I would put it differently:

AI will take a large part of the work humans currently perform inside interfaces.

And the laptop, phone, glasses or local AI system may become a much more powerful extension of the person using it.

A few years from now, the important question may no longer be:

"Which software can you use?"

It may be:

"What part of reality can you organize through it?"

Title: AI is getting a work shift.

Canonical: https://ivankotov.eu/diary/ai-is-getting-a-work-shift/

LinkedIn: https://lnkd.in/p/e-KY-8RY

Raw tags (10): ArtificialIntelligence, AIAgents, HumanAI, Delegation, AIManagement, AIGovernance, FutureOfWork, Automation, AIProductivity, SystemsThinking

### Preserved body

AI is getting a work shift.

A question produces an answer. A delegated task can keep producing actions long after the conversation ends.

That difference matters more than another chat feature.

Once an AI system can continue working while you are elsewhere, the central question changes.

Not just: "How do I ask?" But: "What have I authorized it to do?"

Running a workshop teaches this quickly. You do not give a capable employee instructions for every movement. You agree on the job, the budget, the standard of completion and the point at which they must call you.

AI needs the same operational clarity, without assuming it has a human colleague's judgment.

Consider an overnight assignment: compare suppliers and prepare tomorrow's purchasing decision.

It can collect quotations, check specifications, flag missing information and prepare a recommendation. That does not authorize it to place an order, accept substitute materials or send confidential plans to a new supplier.

The distinction is not between useful autonomy and endless permission requests. It is between decisions already delegated and decisions still reserved.

A useful brief therefore includes more than an objective. It defines permitted actions, resources, evidence of completion and a stopping rule.

Prompt -> task -> bounded execution -> verified result.

And "works around the clock" is not a productivity metric.

An agent that spends the night checking the same sources, launching expensive tools and producing reports nobody needs has not created value. It has automated busyness.

A tireless assistant with vague instructions and access to the company card is not my definition of progress.

I would start with one recurring responsibility. Measure the useful result, the compute bill and the time spent correcting it. Expand the mandate only when the evidence supports it.

The aim is not to approve every click. That would turn the owner into the agent's full-time assistant. The aim is to reserve human attention for the decisions that actually need it.

This is where the next layer of AI literacy may emerge: not simply prompting, but delegation.

Knowing what to hand over. Knowing what must remain yours. Knowing what "done" looks like.

We have spent years learning how to talk to AI.

Now we need to learn how to assign it work without surrendering control of the job.

Title: Intelligence Is Getting Good Enough. Now We Need to Afford It.

Raw tags (6): AI, Inference, AIHardware, TPU, LocalAI, AIInfrastructure

### Preserved body

Intelligence Is Getting Good Enough. Now We Need to Afford It.

Not good enough for everything. Not free of errors. But in my own work, the question is increasingly shifting from “Can AI help solve this?” to “Can I afford to let it work on this for hours?”

The answer cannot always be “use a smaller model”.

Some tasks genuinely need the strongest reasoning available. Downgrading is not efficiency if the savings disappear into retries, mistakes and human repair.

We need to make strong inference cheaper, not merely persuade everyone to accept less intelligence.

A chatbot answers and waits. An agent may plan, call tools, inspect results, revise and try again. Across sequential steps, inference costs accumulate and latency compounds.

That is why I am watching Google's TPUs and purpose-built inference chips such as OpenAI's Jalapeño.

Not because GPUs are obsolete. Because training new models and serving useful intelligence at scale are different workloads. There is no reason to expect one hardware design to be optimal for every combination of capability, latency and cost.

Specialization matters. So does the rest of the machine: memory capacity, bandwidth, interconnects, cooling, utilization and software.

A promising chip is not yet affordable infrastructure. It needs supported models, usable tools and actual access. Better efficiency inside a data center does not automatically become a lower bill for its customers.

And for those of us building locally, the ambition is practical: compact inference systems with enough memory, predictable running costs and software we can actually use.

Not necessarily a miniature training cluster. A machine designed to keep capable models at work.

In a workshop, peak power tells you very little on its own. You ask what the machine produces during a shift, how often it stops and what each finished part costs.

AI deserves the same test.

Not just cheaper tokens. Cheaper completed work, at the required quality and reliability.

For personal AI, coding systems and long-running research, I see affordability as more than convenience. It determines which projects get attempted, how thoroughly they can be checked and who gets to build them.

I still want better models.

But I also want the intelligence we already have to become practical infrastructure, rather than something we ration between impressive demonstrations.

The next breakthrough should not only raise the ceiling of intelligence.

It should lower the cost of keeping it at work.
